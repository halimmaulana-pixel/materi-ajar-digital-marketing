B. Session 1 — Foundations & Scope of Digital Marketing

1. What Is Digital Marketing?

Digital marketing is the set of marketing activities that use electronic channels and the internet to reach, engage, and convert a target audience. Unlike traditional marketing, which is largely one-way (a brand broadcasts a message through television, radio, or print), digital marketing is interactive and measurable: consumers can respond, share, review, and co-create content, while marketers can track behavior in near real time.

Key Term — Detailed Explanation
Digital Marketing
Marketing that uses digital technologies and online channels (websites, search engines, social media, email, mobile apps, marketplaces, and messaging platforms) to promote products or services. Its defining features are interactivity (two-way communication), measurability (actions can be tracked and attributed), targetability (messages can be tailored to segments), and speed (campaigns can be launched and optimized quickly).

2. The POEM Framework: Owned, Earned, Paid Media

The POEM framework classifies every digital touchpoint by who controls it and how attention is obtained. A healthy strategy balances all three: paid drives reach, owned builds long-term assets, and earned provides social proof.

Marketing has evolved as technology changed how people discover, evaluate, and buy. The table below summarizes the shift and the growing role of AI.

Key Term — Detailed Explanation
Marketing 5.0 / Marketing 6.0
Marketing 5.0 (popularized by Philip Kotler and colleagues) describes the application of technologies that mimic human capabilities—AI, natural language processing, sensors, and robotics—to create, communicate, deliver, and enhance value across the customer journey, while keeping a human-centric purpose. Marketing 6.0 extends this into immersive and "phygital" (physical + digital) experiences where humans and intelligent systems collaborate continuously; generative AI and autonomous agents become part of everyday marketing operations.

4. AI as a Structural Element (Not Just a Tool)

In the AI era, Artificial Intelligence is embedded across the entire marketing lifecycle: it collects and unifies data, analyzes patterns at scale, predicts behavior, generates content variations, and optimizes campaigns automatically. Treating AI as "just another tool" underestimates its impact on strategy, organization, and governance.

C. Session 2 — Strategy & Planning

1. SOSTAC: A Structured Planning Framework

SOSTAC provides a logical sequence for building and controlling a digital marketing plan. Each stage answers a specific question, ensuring no critical element is skipped.

Key Term — Detailed Explanation
SOSTAC
A planning framework created by PR Smith consisting of six stages: Situation (where are we now?), Objectives (where do we want to be?), Strategy (how do we get there, broadly?), Tactics (which specific tools and channels?), Action (who does what, when, with which resources?), and Control (how do we measure, monitor, and adjust?). It is valued for its completeness—from analysis to governance.
StageGuiding QuestionTypical OutputsAI Support Example
SituationWhere are we now?SWOT, audience insights, competitor scan, channel auditSocial listening and trend detection at scale
ObjectivesWhere do we want to be?OKRs/KPIs, targets, timelinesForecasting likely outcomes from historical data
StrategyHow do we get there?Segmentation, targeting, positioning, value propositionAudience clustering and propensity modeling
TacticsWhich tools & channels?Channel mix, content plan, ad formatsCreative variant generation, channel recommendations
ActionWho does what, when?RACI, schedules, budgets, workflowsAutomated task routing and scheduling
ControlDid we succeed?Dashboards, reviews, optimization backlogReal-time anomaly detection and budget reallocation

2. RACE: Managing the Customer Lifecycle

Key Term — Detailed Explanation
RACE Framework
A customer-lifecycle framework by Smart Insights: Reach (build awareness and drive traffic), Act (prompt interactions and leads on site or profiles), Convert (turn leads into paying customers), and Engage (build long-term relationships to drive repeat sales and advocacy). Unlike SOSTAC, which structures the whole plan, RACE focuses on what to do at each stage of the customer journey and how to measure it.
Figure 2. The RACE funnel maps customer stages to measurable outcomes. Each stage requires different content and tactics.

3. The Conversion Funnel: TOFU, MOFU, BOFU

TOFU — Top of Funnel (Awareness)
Goal: attract a broad audience and create awareness.
Content: educational blog posts, short videos, social content, infographics.
Metrics: reach, impressions, video views, new visitors.
MOFU — Middle of Funnel (Consideration)
Goal: educate and nurture prospects comparing options.
Content: guides, webinars, case studies, comparison pages, email sequences.
Metrics: leads, downloads, email sign-ups, engagement time.
BOFU — Bottom of Funnel (Decision)
Goal: convert and close the sale.
Content: demos, trials, testimonials, offers, clear checkout.
Metrics: conversion rate, cost per acquisition (CPA), revenue.
Key Term — Detailed Explanation
TOFU / MOFU / BOFU
A funnel metaphor describing stages of customer readiness. Top of Funnel (TOFU) targets people who may not yet know the brand or problem deeply—focus on awareness and value. Middle of Funnel (MOFU) targets people evaluating solutions—focus on trust and differentiation. Bottom of Funnel (BOFU) targets people ready to decide—focus on removing friction and proving value. Effective plans deliver the right message, in the right format, at the right stage.

4. Goals and Measurement: OKR and KPI

ConceptDefinitionStructure / ExamplePurpose
OKR (Objectives and Key Results)A goal-setting method that pairs a qualitative objective with measurable key results.Objective: Grow qualified online demand. KR1: +40% organic sessions. KR2: 5% lead-to-customer rate. KR3: CPA < $12.Align teams on ambitious direction and track progress.
KPI (Key Performance Indicator)A specific metric used to evaluate performance against a target.Conversion rate, CPA, ROAS, CTR, LTV, retention rate.Monitor health and trigger optimization decisions.
Key Term — Detailed Explanation
CPA, LTV, ROAS, CTR
CPA (Cost Per Acquisition): total cost to acquire one customer (spend ÷ conversions). Lower is better if quality holds. LTV (Lifetime Value): the total net revenue expected from a customer over the relationship; must be compared to CPA for sustainability. ROAS (Return on Ad Spend): revenue generated per unit of ad spend (revenue ÷ ad cost). CTR (Click-Through Rate): clicks ÷ impressions; measures ad or link relevance.

5. SWOT in the Digital & AI Era

A digital SWOT analyzes internal strengths/weaknesses and external opportunities/threats with attention to channels, data, and technology. AI can enrich SWOT by processing competitor activity, search trends, and sentiment in near real time—but human judgment must validate insights.

HelpfulHarmful
InternalStrengths: strong owned content, loyal email list, skilled team, clean first-party data.Weaknesses: slow website, weak SEO, no analytics discipline, limited budget.
ExternalOpportunities: rising search demand, new AI discovery surfaces (AI Overviews), emerging creator channels.Threats: platform algorithm changes, privacy regulation, AI-generated competitor content, ad cost inflation.

D. Case Study: Local Clothing Store A vs B

Context. Two local clothing stores target similar customers with comparable budgets. Store A relies on printed catalogs and radio ads. Store B builds a website, manages social media, and uses AI-driven product recommendations based on browsing behavior.

DimensionStore A (Traditional)Store B (Digital + AI)
ChannelsPrint, radio (Paid/Offline)Website, social, email, targeted ads (Owned + Paid + Earned)
TargetingBroad, mass audienceSegmented by behavior and interest
PersonalizationNoneAI product recommendations and tailored messages
MeasurementEstimated, delayedReal-time analytics and conversion tracking
6-Month OutcomeBaseline reach and sales~3× reach and ~2× conversion rate

Lesson. The difference was not simply a larger budget, but the strategic use of digital channels and AI to deliver the right offer to the right audience and to learn quickly from data.

G. Real-World Case Study & Detailed Solution

Case: Domino's Pizza — Turning Around a Failing Brand with Digital Strategy and Data (Reported Real Case)

Background (real, reported problem). By 2008–2009, Domino's Pizza (United States) faced a widely reported brand crisis: customer perception of product quality was poor, same-store sales were under pressure, and the company lagged in digital ordering compared with emerging expectations. Public feedback (including harsh reviews and focus-group reactions) made the problem visible. Domino's leadership chose a transparent "Oh Yes We Did" turnaround, reformulated the product, and—critically for this course—rebuilt the business around digital channels, measurable goals, and technology (online/mobile ordering, pizza tracker, and later AI-assisted ordering and voice). This is a documented transformation frequently cited in marketing and business case literature.

The problem statement. "How can a legacy pizza chain with weak brand perception and low digital maturity regain relevance, grow orders, and build a measurable, customer-centric marketing system in an increasingly digital (and later AI-enabled) market?"

Step-by-Step Reasoning (Why this solves the stated problem)

  1. Diagnose honestly (Situation): the core issues were trust/perception and convenience. Ignoring perception would make any ad spend inefficient.
  2. Commit to measurable change (Objectives): shifting targets toward digital order share and repeat behavior focused the organization on outcomes, not vanity metrics.
  3. Build the owned hub (Strategy/Tactics): investing in app/site and the tracker created a controlled, measurable customer experience—improving both convenience (TOFU→BOFU conversion) and data capture.
  4. Use paid & earned to reset perception (POEM): transparent messaging addressed the trust deficit, while earned conversation amplified credibility when the product improved.
  5. Optimize the lifecycle (RACE/Control): tracking each stage revealed drop-offs; iterative fixes (UX, offers, timing) compounded into sustained growth and higher LTV.

Key lesson for Sessions 1–2: digital marketing success came not from a single channel but from an integrated, measured system—clear objectives, the right POEM mix, funnel-stage execution (RACE), and continuous control—later strengthened by data/AI capabilities.
